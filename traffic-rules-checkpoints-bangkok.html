Bangkok Traffic Rules for Motorbike Riders: Checkpoints, Helmets & Fines

The rules that actually get enforced in Bangkok are a shorter list than you'd think. Here's what to expect at a checkpoint and how to avoid the fines tourists get most often.

The helmet law (and why it's the one that matters most)

Helmets are legally required for both rider and passenger, and it's the single rule police enforce most consistently on foreign riders — more than lane discipline, more than speed. Every Roadmates rental includes a helmet for exactly this reason; wear it every ride, even for a five-minute trip to 7-Eleven, since that's exactly when riders get complacent and exactly when checkpoints tend to be set up.

What to expect at a police checkpoint

Checkpoints are common around holidays, long weekends, and in tourist-dense areas, usually a couple of officers waving riders to the roadside. They'll typically ask for your license and the bike's registration document (kept in the under-seat compartment or handed over at pickup). Stay calm, hand over both documents, and answer basic questions — most stops for compliant riders take under five minutes.

Common fines for tourists

The fines we see riders get most often, in order: no helmet, riding without a valid license (a Category-A endorsed IDP or Thai license), and riding the wrong way down a one-way soi. All three are entirely avoidable — wear the helmet we give you, bring proper documents (see our license requirements guide), and check street signs or Google Maps before turning down a narrow soi.

Lane rules at big junctions

Thailand drives on the left, and lane discipline is looser in practice than in Western cities — expect scooters filtering between cars, and it's normal (and expected) to do the same at a stop. At major junctions, turning right often means queuing in a separate, painted motorbike box or lane rather than crossing traffic directly like a car would — watch for the road markings before committing to a turn, especially at big intersections in Sathorn and Silom.

One-way streets and expressway restrictions

Many of Bangkok's narrower sois are one-way, and going the wrong direction is both dangerous and one of the more common tourist fines — Google Maps' two-wheeler routing mode respects these correctly, so use it rather than guessing. Elevated expressways (tollways) are off-limits to most rental scooters regardless of engine size; plan surface-street routes instead, which is almost always fine since expressways rarely save meaningful time within the city anyway.

If you're stopped: what to actually do

Pull over calmly, keep your documents accessible (not buried in a backpack), and be polite — most officers dealing with tourists just want compliance, not confrontation. If you genuinely don't understand a question, "sorry, no Thai" (พูดไทยไม่ได้ครับ/ค่ะ) and a patient attitude go a long way.
